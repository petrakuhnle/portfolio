Master-Follower

The problem

Requirements engineers write each requirement in a fixed form, like a clause in a standard: “The wave height shall be measured with an accuracy of at least 0.30 m.” The same requirement is often reused across projects.

When the original changes, every copy may need to change too. Before, a decision made in a meeting was noted in a document, sent by email and updated by hand, or missed.

Who needed what

  • Master editor. Changes the source requirement and needs to know who is affected
  • Follower owner. Needs to see a change and decide whether to apply it
  • Manager signing off. Needs to see what is in sync and what has diverged

The shipped feature

A change on the Master flags every Follower. Its owner applies or rejects it, and the decision is logged.

  • Masterthe source
  • Up to datein sync
  • Change pendingneeds review
  • Rejected or editeddiverged

Key design decisions

My job was to ask what each person needs to see and what would frustrate them.

  • Flags, not silent updates. Only the owner applies, rejects or edits a change
  • A state per Follower. A change left pending or rejected can still be acted on later
  • Discussion in place. Decisions are made where the work is
  • Options where each role needs them. Assign, review and comment appear in the same table for every role

Process

The founders had a title and a high-level idea, but no specs, no user stories and no sketch. I started there and built interactive hi-fi prototypes, so clients and internal experts could react to an almost real product instead of imagining one. We iterated several times a week, and I introduced user stories along the way as a second source of truth.

Because I was involved from the first conversation to the release, I caught a miscommunication between the CPO and the backend engineers about what the feature should do now and later, and prevented rework in the code.

Lessons learned

What the first big feature taught me.

  1. Design filled the gap

    Engineers could build anything, but couldn't picture the feature in enough detail. The prototypes turned high-level decisions into logic developers could build.

  2. A missing middle step

    More user testing and a feasibility check between prototype and build would have helped. That was partly startup reality and partly outside my influence.

  3. Carried into the next feature

    On the next feature I asked for user stories and the most important requirements to be agreed from the start. See Verification Assignment Matrix.
