Master-Follower

Requirements reused across projects, traceable when they change. Designed in 2021, live in Altium 365 today.

Context
First designer in an engineering-led hardware startup, in a domain new to me
Scope
Discovery to tested hi-fi prototype
Users
Four engineer roles and managers signing off
Outcome
One of three features behind a six-figure development contract

Impact

With this feature I started the company's first UX practice. Interviews, journeys and prototypes became a normal way of working. Showing designs back to the same experts a week later made me listen closely, and I caught a detail that prevented a development refactor.

The shipped feature

Systems engineers work with requirements. When one changes, others across projects may need to change too. Master-Follower links them: one Master, many Followers. The shipped feature still works as I designed it in 2021. A backend developer later told me improvements were easy to add because the logic was mapped out well.

  • Masterthe source
  • Up to datein sync
  • Change pendingneeds review
  • Rejected or editeddiverged

How it works

A change on the Master flags every Follower. Its owner applies or rejects it, and the decision is logged.

Each Follower keeps its own state, so a change left pending or rejected can still be acted on. Discussion happens in place. Before, a decision made in a meeting was noted in a document, sent by email and updated by hand, or missed.

Process

Interviews with pro users, notes and user stories, a user journey, wireframes to hi-fi, and testing with experts and users.

Company
Valispace GmbH · acquired by Altium 2023
Team
CPO · subject-matter experts · CTO · designer owning the design process
My role
Discovery to hi-fi prototype · user stories · logic mapping · testing

The Master's editor, the Follower's owner and the manager signing off need different things from one table. Options to assign, review and comment appear where each role needs them.

Key design decisions

My job was to ask what each person needs to see and what would frustrate them. I worked from the user and visual side so developers could focus on the technical side.

  • Flags, not silent updates. Only the owner applies, rejects or edits a change
  • Discussion in place. Decisions are made where the work is
  • Readable by every role. Engineers and managers signing off use the same table
  • Prototype as alignment. Experts, users and deciders agreed before the build

Lessons learned

What the first big feature taught me.

  1. Design filled the gap

    Interviews and user stories turned high-level feature decisions into logic developers could build.

  2. A missing middle step

    More user testing and a feasibility check between prototype and build would have helped. That was partly startup reality and partly outside my influence.

  3. Carried into the next feature

    On the next feature I asked for user stories and the most important requirements to be agreed from the start. See Verification Assignment Matrix.
