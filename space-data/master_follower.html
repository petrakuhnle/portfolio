Master–Follower

Requirements reused across projects, traceable when they change. Valispace 2021, live in Altium 365 today.

Context
First designer in an engineering-led hardware startup, six years in, domain new to me.
Scope
Discovery to tested hi-fi prototype, owned end to end.
Users
Four engineer roles plus managers signing off. Validated with internal experts and external users.
Outcome
One of three features behind a six-figure contract. Shipped 2021, kept through the Altium acquisition, publicly documented.

After I started the company's first UX practice, discovery through interviews, journeys and prototypes became a normal way of working. The company moved toward product-led decisions.

Because I had to design what I was told and show it back to the same experts a week later, I paid closer attention than listening alone allows. Later I caught a detail that prevented a development refactor.

The shipped feature

Engineers building hardware like aeroplanes work with requirements, above all requirements and systems engineers. To manage the impact of one changing requirement on others across projects, we built the Master–Follower feature. One Master, many Followers. The shipped design works in essence as I designed it in 2021. Later iterations added functionality and improved usability. A backend developer who updated the feature later told me improvements were easy to implement because the design logic was mapped out well.

The states used to manage and track interdependent changes

How it works

A change on the Master flags every Follower. Its owner applies or rejects it, and the decision is logged.

A change on the Master reaches every Follower automatically, as a flag to review. A key decision was to allow different states, synced, pending, rejected, so a change left pending too long, or rejected, can still be acted on. Everyone involved can also discuss changes and decisions in place.

Key design decisions

My job was to ask the specific questions: what does the engineer working on a Follower need to see, what would frustrate them, what does the Master's editor need versus the Follower's owner? Same user focus, but for different people in different situations. I build from the user and visual side so developers can focus on the technical side, and we meet in the middle. This role-and-view thinking is taken furthest in Use Case 3, Review Center.

Lessons learned

What the first big feature taught me.

  1. Design filled the gap

    I built the logic from interviews and user stories. Design let us align earlier on detail developers couldn't guess. It filled the gap between high-level feature decisions and development.

  2. A missing middle step

    Ideally there would have been a middle step, more user testing and detailed feasibility alignment, rather than going prototype → contract → build. Part startup reality, part outside my influence at the time.

  3. Carried into the next feature

    On the next feature I asked for user-story collaboration and agreement on the most crucial requirements from the start. See Use Case 2, Verification Assignment Matrix.
