02 · The shipped feature

Engineers building hardware like aeroplanes work with requirements, above all requirements and systems engineers. To manage the impact of one changing requirement on others across projects, we built the Master–Follower feature. One Master, many Followers. The shipped design works in essence as I designed it in 2021. Later iterations added functionality and improved usability. A backend developer who updated the feature later told me improvements were easy to implement because the design logic was mapped out well.

03 · How it works

A change on the Master flags every Follower for its owner to apply or reject, with the decision logged.

A change on the Master reaches every Follower automatically, as a flag to review, not as a silent update. A key decision was to allow different states, synced, pending, rejected, so a change left pending too long, or rejected, can still be acted on. Everyone involved can also discuss changes and decisions in place.

05 · Key design decisions

My job was to ask the specific questions: what does the engineer working on a Follower need to see, what would frustrate them, what does the Master's editor need versus the Follower's owner? Same user focus, but for different people in different situations. I build from the user and visual side so developers can focus on the technical side, and we meet in the middle. This role-and-view thinking is taken furthest in Use Case 3, Review Center.

02 · UX Founding Work

How I introduced UX to an engineering-led startup

Valispace, 2021–2022, through the company's growth phase from 20 to 50 people. I built the practice while designing the shipped features.

Show, don't tell

Founded the UX Guild for designers and developers, and introduced user research and prototype testing by starting with the people who were open to it. I took every contribution seriously and kept findings specific, so the method did the arguing for me.

One look and feel

As the only designer, consistency across surfaces was mine by default. I set the design principles and carried one look and feel across product, API documentation and marketing, with marketing, solution management and development: input on the website, colour combinations for marketing resources, consistency in the API design, feedback on visuals in the docs. Priorities shifted weekly, so deciding what mattered most was part of the job. The core stayed on system of systems features, the design culture, and getting a second designer hired.

From UX to Product

As the company moved to a product led approach, engineering, product and design started owning the experience together. I was promoted onto the new product team, working with PMs, dev leads, the CTO and the CPO. I defined the senior role for the second hire and hired my successor, who still shapes the design culture at Altium today.

Accessibility as a requirement

I made the case for improving the colour palette of a pie chart on contrast grounds. Months later, users reported exactly the contrast problem I had flagged. Meeting accessibility guidelines keeps expert users working instead of reporting UI they cannot read or operate.

Owning systemic problems

I took ownership of systemic problems no one had framed yet, in a product where every feature touches a system of systems. I brought engineers, developers and end users into the same conversation and translated between what was technically possible and what customers would pay for, holding usability and business needs together rather than trading one for the other.

Learning the domain

I came to aerospace from four years at Apple, in daily contact with professional audio and video users. I diagnosed problems in live production, stayed with the person until I understood their situation, and wrote up reproducible cases for engineering, deciding case by case what was a bug and what was a UX problem. That is how I learned systems engineering: isolate the variable, reproduce it with the person who has it, and separate what is broken from what is badly designed.
